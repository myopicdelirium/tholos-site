A1 is the simplest case: one agent, a moisture field, and water. It was tempting
to call it "the agent learns to find water." This diagnostic tests that claim
directly, and rejects it.

![A1 learning × gradient 2×2](/figures/a1_learning_gradient_2x2.svg)

## The 2×2

Two factors, crossed, 20 seeds per cell:

- **learning**: on, or frozen (`learning.enabled: false` — the within-lifetime
  Q-value updates never fire).
- **gradient**: on, or off (`fields.moisture.enabled: false` — the water sources
  still exist, but there is no moisture gradient to read).

| | gradient on | gradient off |
|---|---|---|
| **learning on** | 100% survive · median 1500 | 20% survive · median 100 |
| **learning frozen** | 100% survive · median 1500 | 20% survive · median 100 |

## The reading

The rows are identical; the columns differ. Freezing learning moves survival by
**zero** — in both gradient conditions the frozen row equals the learning row.
Removing the gradient is what collapses survival, from 100% to a 20% luck floor
(the fraction of seeds that spawn close enough to stumble onto water before
thirst kills them at ~tick 100).

So A1's within-lifetime learning is **parasitic on perception**: it can only
sharpen what the gradient already points toward; it cannot substitute for it.
Turn the gradient off and there is nothing for learning to sharpen.

## Why this matters

A1 is therefore a **reactive baseline** — the control that shows what pure
perception-plus-reflex achieves. That is exactly what we want it to be: when
learning moves outcomes in a later case, we can prove it *by contrast* against
this null, rather than assuming it. Naming A1 as reactive now keeps us honest
later.
